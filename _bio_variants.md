## Website Bio (current, March 2026)

**Otto Kienitz** holds a Ph.D. in Political Science from the University of California, Berkeley.

His research interests include democracy and autocracy, state-building and state capacity, and local taxation and representation in comparative perspective. He is an expert in Russian politics, including local politics, public goods provision, and the historical political economy of the Russian Empire.

His book manuscript, *Between State and Democracy*, asks why rulers who faced no democratic pressure nevertheless created local elected assemblies and gave them power over taxation. The answer, he argues, is that these institutions solved a problem no bureaucracy could: getting local elites to reveal accurate information about wealth and pay their share. The book traces how institutions designed for extraction ended up generating public goods, building human capital, and creating pressure for broader political inclusion — across nineteenth-century Europe, Eurasia, and beyond.

His other work explores the political economy of state-building from several directions: how landowning elites responded to decentralization by capturing local bureaucracies (with Igor Kolesnikov), what state capacity actually means and how to study it as a policy problem rather than a single variable (with Ruth Collier), and how proximity to national borders shapes political violence and identity (with Robert Braun).

## Cover Letter / Formal (LSE post-doc, 2026)

My research sits at the intersection of empirical political science and public policy, drawing on comparative evidence from historical political economy. I study why states so often fail to deliver public goods and services, how decentralization and democratization reforms reshape the local political arena, and what factors allow weak states to incrementally build local state capacity over time.

My book manuscript, *Between State and Democracy*, asks a question at the heart of both comparative politics and political economy: why autocrats voluntarily create local democratic institutions, and under what conditions these institutions help build state capacity? I argue that local democratization was a mechanism for state-capacity building: a solution to a fiscal agency problem in which states could not trust local elites to assess and collect taxes on themselves. Elected assemblies incentivized elite participation in the fiscal system while holding them accountable for providing accurate information that no central bureaucracy could extract as effectively. Drawing on original data across over 400 local districts within nineteenth-century Russia, I show that these assemblies generated measurable investments in fiscal capacity, but that elite capture and public goods provision were not opposed: they formed two faces of the same institutional logic.

## Technical / Academic (for specialist audiences)

His book manuscript argues that states dependent on elite-controlled fiscal intermediation could not shift to direct taxation without destroying existing revenue. The surtax mechanism — whereby local bodies piggy-backed assessments on central tax rolls — solved this agency problem, but only when democratic features (broader franchise, peer monitoring, information competition) made assessment credible. Institutions created for extraction generated public goods, human capital, and pressure for inclusion: the extractive origins of local democracy.

The argument is developed in three layers. Layer 1 establishes the fiscal intermediation bind. Layer 2 identifies the surtax mechanism as the institutional solution. Layer 3 traces the dynamic by which extractive institutions generated democratic accountability as an emergent outcome.

## Short Bio (1 paragraph, for conference programs etc.)

Otto Kienitz is a Post-Doctoral Fellow at the SNF Agora Institute at Johns Hopkins University. He holds a Ph.D. in Political Science from UC Berkeley. His research examines how weak states build capacity through local democratic institutions, with a focus on the historical political economy of taxation, representation, and public goods provision. His book manuscript, *Between State and Democracy*, studies why rulers across nineteenth-century Europe and Eurasia created local elected assemblies and empowered them with fiscal authority — and how these institutions reshaped state-society relations from the ground up.

## Original Dissertation Bio (pre-2025, archived)

His Ph.D. dissertation examines local democratization as state-building, identifying the relationship between local taxation and representation in weak states such as the nineteenth century Russian Empire, Ottoman Empire, and Qing Dynasty in historical comparative perspective. His other work explores the historical political economy of state-building with a focus on the role of local elites in constraining or enabling state-building and the development of state capacity over time and space.
